// WHO definition

Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events (including disease), and the application of this study to the control of diseases and other health problems.

Who gets it, how often, where and when – the frequency and pattern of health events in a population.
The factors that bring about a change in a health condition: why it happens. Where we live, environment, genetics, income, education, relationships.
Diseases, injuries, birth defects, maternal and child health, occupational health, environmental health. Falls are an injury.
The point is action: findings are used to prevent and control health problems.

// Epidemiology is a science

It relies on a working knowledge of probability, statistics and sound research methods. It is a method of causal reasoning: develop and test hypotheses to explain health-related behaviours, states and events.

// What counts as "disease"?

A broad concept: physiological or psychological dysfunction (communicable or not), plus health-related problems such as smoking, a sedentary lifestyle or high-fat diets, and the antecedents of social problems such as child abuse, violence or crime.

LESSON 1 · KEY CONCEPTS

Distribution & Determinants

Two questions drive epidemiology: what is the pattern? and what is behind it?

// Distribution = frequency + pattern

Frequency relates the number of health events to the size of the population – which lets us compare across populations.

Pattern is occurrence by TIME, PLACE and PERSON.

Example: during the pandemic an IMH study found 8.7% of surveyed Singaporeans met criteria for clinical depression, 9.4% for anxiety, and 9.3% for mild to severe stress. These are proportions, not raw counts.

// Time · Place · Person

Trends, seasons, epidemic curves.
Falls: are fall injuries rising year on year as the population ages?
Country, region, neighbourhood, setting.
Falls: WHO reports 60% of deaths in the Western Pacific and South-East Asia regions.
Age, sex, occupation, living arrangement.
Falls: adults over 60 have the highest fatal-fall rates.

LESSON 1 · PART II

Epidemiology & Public Health

// Public health

All organised measures (public or private) to prevent disease, promote health and prolong life among the population as a whole.

Epidemiology is an integral component of public health: it provides the foundation for practical, science-based public health action.

Behavioural science / health educationBiostatisticsEnvironmental healthEpidemiologyHealth services managementGlobal healthMaternal & child healthNutritionLaboratory practicePolicy

// Roles of epidemiology

Epidemiological findings help prevent and control the occurrence and spread of disease, and prevent injury, disability and death – by informing public health policy and planning.
What are the actual and potential problems? Where? Which populations are at increased risk? Which problems are declining or increasing? How do patterns relate to the level and distribution of health services?
Epidemiologists add to clinicians' understanding of the natural history of disease, e.g. notifying authorities of unusual clinical presentations in new COVID-19 strains.
Research that identifies the causal factors affecting risk. Example: the 1976 American Legion Convention pneumonia outbreak in Philadelphia, which revealed a variety of risk factors.
Epidemiologic methods apply to personal choices. News headline: "Teens who spend too much time on the internet at risk of high blood pressure." How much time should I spend online?
LESSON 2 · PART I

Historical Evolution

Tap a node on the timeline.

Hippocrates · environment matters

Linked disease to environment – "Airs, Waters, Places". An early idea that place and surroundings are determinants.

John Graunt · counting the dead

Analysed London's Bills of Mortality: the birth of vital statistics and population-level pattern-finding.

Edward Jenner · prevention before disease

Smallpox vaccination – a landmark in primary prevention.

Ignaz Semmelweis · handwashing

Showed hand disinfection cut maternal deaths in the clinic – a comparison of outcomes under different practices.

John Snow · the Broad Street pump

Mapped cholera deaths in London, tracing them to one contaminated water pump. Descriptive epidemiology by place and a policy action (removing the pump handle).

Pasteur & Koch · germ theory

Specific microorganisms cause specific diseases – replacing the idea of "bad air" (miasma) and enabling targeted control.

Framingham, Doll & Hill · beyond infection

Epidemiology turned to chronic disease: heart-disease risk factors (Framingham Heart Study) and smoking linked to lung cancer – analytic studies with comparison groups.

Smallpox eradication

Surveillance, field investigation and vaccination working together – a global public health triumph.

COVID-19 · real-time epidemiology

Live surveillance, genomic tracking, spot-the-cluster field work and policy on distance, duration and ventilation.

// Germ theory: what changed?

Disease came from foul air and bad environments. Action: clean up smells, general sanitation.
Specific microbes cause specific diseases. Action: identify the agent, interrupt transmission, vaccinate, treat. It reshaped how we investigate and control disease.
LESSON 2 · PART II

Core Epidemiological Functions

Tap a hexagon.

Public health surveillance

The ongoing, systematic collection, analysis and interpretation of health-related data essential to planning, implementation and evaluation of public health practice.
Falls: hospital admissions, emergency visits, death registries, screening data.

Field investigation

Identify the source and/or cause of a problem. Outbreaks start with descriptive epidemiology: incidence and distribution by time, place and person, calculating rates and finding higher-risk groups (e.g. the spot map of SARS cases on the 9th floor of the Metropole Hotel, Hong Kong, 2003).
Falls: a cluster of falls in one care facility – what do the locations, times and residents have in common?

Analytic studies

Surveillance and field work generate hypotheses; analytic studies test them. The hallmark is a valid comparison group (case vs control; treated vs untreated).
Falls: do seniors who do balance training fall less than those who do not?

Evaluation

Did the programme or policy work? Measure its impact against its goals, then improve it.
Falls: did the screening and exercise programme reduce fall injuries?

Linkages

Epidemiology works with clinicians, labs, communities, other sectors and data systems so findings reach where they can act.
Falls: link clinic, community centre, family and home-design data.

Policy development

Turn evidence into guidance, standards and regulation.
Falls: building standards, home-modification support, staffing ratios in care facilities.

// Descriptive vs analytical

Descriptive asks "what?" – it describes something.

Analytical asks "why?" – how did it come to be?

03

Think like an epidemiologist

Discuss each prompt with your team first, then open it to see the epidemiology lens.

Who is affected, where and when? DISTRIBUTION
Describe the case by person (age, living situation), place (where the fall happened) and time (time of day, season, trend). To compare communities fairly you need rates (events per population), not just counts.
What made a fall more likely? DETERMINANTS
Determinants sit at several layers: the individual (balance, strength, medication), behaviour (activity, footwear), the physical environment (stairs, lighting, floors), the social context (living alone, caregiver support, income) and the health system and policy (screening, access, building standards).
How would we know the size of the problem? SURVEILLANCE
Ongoing, systematic collection of fall-related admissions, emergency visits and deaths – plus risk-screening data. This is the evidence that tells us whether the number of falls is growing or shrinking.
How would we learn why some people fall and others do not? ANALYTIC
Compare groups: fallers vs non-fallers, or those who joined a balance programme vs those who did not. A valid comparison group is the hallmark of an analytic study.
Where could we have stopped it? PREVENTION
Primary: make homes safer, build strength and balance before any fall. Secondary: detect raised fall risk early through screening and act on it. Tertiary: rehabilitation and support after an injury to prevent further falls and disability.
Why is a number like 684,000 a public health failure we can fix? ACTION
Because the determinants are largely modifiable. WHO lists evidence-based measures: gait, balance and functional training, Tai Chi, home assessment and modification, reviewing medications, multifactorial interventions with individual risk assessment, and better building and workplace standards. Epidemiology turns that evidence into targeted action.
